When a Christian mom asks me what makes a nature study curriculum truly Christian, I tell her this: we start from the Creator, and we end at His provision. We don't start with nature. Nature is a byproduct of an intentional God.

That one difference changes everything about how a child looks at a leaf.

## Start with the Creator, not the creation

A lot of nature study begins with the plant and stops there. The flower is beautiful, the bug is fascinating, and the wonder just hangs in the air with nowhere to go.

Scripture gives that wonder a direction. Paul writes that "all things have been created through Him and for Him. He is before all things, and in Him all things hold together" (Colossians 1:16-17, NASB). The plant didn't make itself, and it isn't the point of the lesson. It's evidence of Someone.

So in Eden's Table, every week opens with God. On Monday we read a story or a passage of Scripture before we ever pick up the herb. By the time your child holds that week's plant, they already know Who made it.

## End at His provision

Then we go all the way to the other end: what did God make this for? From the very beginning He told us, "Behold, I have given you every plant yielding seed that is on the surface of all the earth" (Genesis 1:29, NASB). He put us in a garden, and everything He does is intentional.

That's why the middle of our week is so practical. Your kids smell and sketch the plant, they make something with it in the kitchen, and they learn where it grows and how people have used it through history. The goal isn't to worship the creation. It's to look at the provision and give glory to God for it.

## The quiet problem with "neutral" nature study

When I was taking classes on plants myself, I kept running into the same thing. Either the material never touched the spiritual at all, as if nothing exists that can't be seen under a microscope, or it went right up to the edge. It would talk about the "intelligence" of a plant or a "life force" that animates it, and then give the credit to nature itself or to the universe.

They got so close. If there's intelligence in a plant, there's an intelligent Designer. Without naming Him, it quietly becomes a worship of creation, and as a Christian, that didn't sit right with me. So I wanted a nature study that simply names Him.

Romans 1:20 says God's "invisible attributes, that is, His eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived, being understood by what has been made." A Christian nature study helps a child see exactly that.

## What slowing down teaches

Eden's Table spends a whole week on one plant, and that's on purpose. Kids today are used to everything being instant. A week with one plant teaches them to slow down, take a breath and observe.

And when they slow down, they notice the details: a square stem, the way leaves are arranged, the order and precision in something small. Think about walking into a home where every little thing was thought about. Nature is no different. Every little thing was thought about, and through that, I want kids to feel loved by a Designer who thought these things through for them.

## What to look for in a Christian nature study curriculum

If you're comparing options, here are the questions I'd ask:

- **Where does the wonder go?** Does the lesson name the Creator, or does it credit "nature" or "the universe"?
- **Does it start with God?** Scripture should be the foundation of the week, not a verse tacked on at the end.
- **Does it end at provision?** Kids should come away knowing what God made the plant for, and grateful for it.
- **Does it slow down?** One thing studied closely beats ten things skimmed.
- **Can you actually teach it?** You shouldn't need a botany degree or an hour of prep. Look for something you can open and learn alongside your child.
