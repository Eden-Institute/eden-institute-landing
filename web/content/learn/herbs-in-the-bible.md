When you look for herbs in the Bible for healing, you find something bigger than a list of remedies. Scripture never treats plants as an accident. From the first chapter of Genesis to the last chapter of Revelation, God puts plants into the hands of His people on purpose: to feed them, to tend their wounds, and to point them back to Him. The healing herbs in the Bible are not a footnote. They are part of His design, and they show us how intentional He was.

## God gave us herbs: Genesis 1:29 and Psalm 104:14

Before anyone was sick, God had already provided. On the sixth day He said, "Behold, I have given you every plant yielding seed that is on the surface of all the earth, and every tree which has fruit yielding seed; it shall be food for you" (Genesis 1:29, NASB). The King James Version says it the way many of us first learned it: "I have given you every herb bearing seed" (Genesis 1:29, KJV).

The psalmist says the same thing about God's ongoing care: "He causes the grass to grow for the cattle, And vegetation for the labor of mankind, So that they may produce food from the earth" (Psalm 104:14, NASB). In the KJV, God causes "herb for the service of man" to grow.

Notice who is doing the giving. God doesn't stumble onto plants and decide to make use of them. He makes them, He gives them, and He hands them to people on purpose. Everything else in this article grows out of that.

## Hezekiah healed with figs: the fig poultice in 2 Kings 20

The clearest healing remedy in all of Scripture is a fig poultice.

King Hezekiah was sick enough to die. He prayed, and God sent Isaiah back with an answer: "I have heard your prayer, I have seen your tears; behold, I am going to heal you" (2 Kings 20:5, NASB). Then, two verses later: "Then Isaiah said, 'Take a cake of figs.' And they took it and placed it on the inflamed spot, and he recovered" (2 Kings 20:7, NASB). Isaiah tells the same story: "Have them take a cake of figs and apply it to the boil, so that he may recover" (Isaiah 38:21, NASB).

Look at the order. God promises the healing first. Then He uses a fig. The plant didn't replace God, and God didn't skip the plant. He chose to work through something He had made.

History lines up with the text. The Roman writer Pliny recorded that figs, "Boiled in wine, and applied topically, they bring carbuncles to a head" (Natural History, Bostock and Riley translation, 1855). King's American Dispensatory was still recommending roasted or boiled figs as a poultice for boils in 1898.

## Balm of Gilead in the Bible: "Is there no balm in Gilead?"

Balm of Gilead was a real resin, traded across the ancient world. Joseph was sold to a caravan carrying it (Genesis 37:25), and Jacob sent it to Egypt as a gift (Genesis 43:11).

Jeremiah treats it as the obvious remedy, the thing you would reach for: "Bring balm for her pain; Perhaps she may be healed" (Jeremiah 51:8, NASB). And when he grieves over his people, he asks, "Is there no balm in Gilead? Is there no physician there? Why then has not the health of the daughter of my people been restored?" (Jeremiah 8:22, NASB).

That question only works if everyone listening knew balm helped. Pliny described it as "very beneficial to the eyesight" (Natural History, 1855). But Jeremiah also shows its limit. To Egypt he says, "Go up to Gilead and obtain balm... You have used many remedies in vain; There is no healing for you" (Jeremiah 46:11, NASB). Remedies are a gift. They were never meant to replace the One who gives them.

## Oil and wine: the Good Samaritan and anointing the sick

When Jesus describes what love looks like, He describes a man tending wounds with what he had in his bag. The Samaritan "came to him and bandaged up his wounds, pouring oil and wine on them; and he put him on his own animal, and brought him to an inn and took care of him" (Luke 10:34, NASB).

Paul gives Timothy practical advice too: "Do not go on drinking only water, but use a little wine for the sake of your stomach and your frequent ailments" (1 Timothy 5:23, NASB).

And oil shows up alongside prayer. The disciples "were anointing with oil many sick people and healing them" (Mark 6:13, NASB). James tells the church: "Is anyone among you sick? Then he must call for the elders of the church and they are to pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord" (James 5:14, NASB).

Oil and wine came from olive trees and grapevines, two of the most common plants in Israel. God didn't ask His people to find something rare. He used what was already growing around them.

## Leaves for the healing of the nations: what it means

The Bible ends where it began, with trees.

Ezekiel sees a river flowing from the temple, lined with trees: "Their leaves will not wither and their fruit will not fail... their fruit will be for food and their leaves for healing" (Ezekiel 47:12, NASB).

John sees the same picture at the very end of Scripture: "On either side of the river was the tree of life, bearing twelve kinds of fruit, yielding its fruit every month; and the leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations" (Revelation 22:2, NASB).

These passages don't name a species, and they aren't a recipe. They are a promise. In the beginning, God gave plants for food. In the end, He shows us leaves for healing. From the first page to the last, the plants are His provision.

## Other herbs mentioned in the Bible

The healing passages are only part of it. Scripture names many more herbs and spices. Jesus mentions "mint and dill and cumin" (Matthew 23:23, NASB) and "mint, rue, and every kind of garden herb" (Luke 11:42, NASB). David prays, "Purify me with hyssop" (Psalm 51:7, NASB). The wise men brought "gold, frankincense, and myrrh" (Matthew 2:11, NASB), and Nicodemus brought "a mixture of myrrh and aloes" for Jesus' burial (John 19:39, NASB). Coriander, cinnamon, cassia, saffron, garlic and onion all appear too. God filled His Word with the plants He filled His world with.

## What does the Bible say about herbal medicine?

When we use a plant God made, we are not stepping outside of faith. We are receiving something He gave on purpose. The plant doesn't heal by its own power, and it doesn't deserve our trust. God does. The fig, the balm, the oil and the wine all point back to the Giver.
