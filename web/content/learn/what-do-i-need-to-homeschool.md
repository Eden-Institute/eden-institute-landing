To homeschool, you need to follow your state's homeschool law, formally withdraw your child if they're in school now, and keep a few simple records. You don't need a teaching degree, and you don't have to sign up with a school. You're also far from alone: according to EdChoice, nearly 1 in 20 students in America is now homeschooled.

## Is homeschooling legal in every state?

Yes. In HSLDA's words, "Homeschooling is legal in all 50 states." But every state sets its own rules, and HSLDA sorts them into four groups:

- **No notice.** You don't have to notify anyone.
- **Low regulation.** You send a notice to your local school district.
- **Moderate regulation.** You send a notice plus test scores or a professional evaluation of your child's progress.
- **High regulation.** You send a notice or test scores or an evaluation, plus other requirements, like curriculum approval, teacher qualifications for parents or home visits.

Find your state on [HSLDA's legal map](https://hslda.org/legal) and read exactly what it asks of you before you start.

## What is homeschooling, and do I have to enroll somewhere?

Homeschooling is parent directed and primarily home based. You're the one ultimately responsible for your child's education, and the learning doesn't have to happen only at home.

You don't enroll in homeschooling the way you enroll in a school. You follow your state's homeschool law, which might mean sending a notice to your school district, or nothing at all. Your state's page on HSLDA's legal map spells out what's required.

One thing that trips up new moms: **an online public school or virtual charter school isn't the same as homeschooling.** It's a public school program your child does at home. Even HSLDA limits its legal help to private homeschooling and says disputes involving public charter programs are "not within our focus."

## How do I take my child out of school?

If your child is in a public or private school now, HSLDA's order is:

1. **Meet your state's legal requirements first.** Send any notice your state requires to set up your homeschool.
2. **Then formally withdraw your child.** HSLDA recommends sending correspondence with school officials by certified mail with a return receipt requested.
3. **Keep copies of everything:** the withdrawal letter, your notice and any other paperwork.

If you're starting after the school year ends, HSLDA suggests withdrawing before the next school year begins.

## Do any states pay you to homeschool?

Some states will help pay, through education savings accounts (ESAs). An ESA puts public education money into an account your family spends on approved expenses. EdChoice counts 21 ESA programs in 18 states, and the rules vary a lot:

- In Texas, homeschooled students can take part, but their accounts are capped at $2,000, while most participants get about $10,500.
- In Iowa, students must enroll in an accredited private school, and homeschoolers can't take part.

There's another side to weigh. HSLDA opposes ESAs for homeschoolers and supports an education tax credit instead, warning that "the person who provides the funding will want to control how it's used." So read your program's rules before you sign up, and decide what's right for your family.

Look up your state on [EdChoice's ESA page](https://www.edchoice.org/school-choice/types-of-school-choice/education-savings-account/). If you already have an ESA, Eden's Table is an approved or registered vendor in seven programs, and the [ESA page](/esa) shows how buying works in each state.

## What records and supplies do I need?

### Records

Your state may require specific records, so check its law first. HSLDA suggests keeping:

- **Attendance**, if your state requires it. Attendance days are the days your child is actually doing school.
- **A list of the curriculum and books** you used.
- **Work samples**, often kept in a portfolio. HSLDA suggests a three-year cycle: this year's work plus samples from the two years before.
- **Test scores** from any required tests.
- **All correspondence with school officials**, including notices of intent.
- **Birth certificate and immunization records.**

Keep test scores or portfolios from state-required end-of-year assessments, and all correspondence with school officials, permanently.

A dated notebook in your child's own handwriting covers a lot of this. The Eden's Table Student Notebook works that way: by the end of the year it's a full year of drawings, lab observations, copywork and review, in order.

#### What are the homeschool styles, from least to most structured?

- **Unschooling.** Child-led learning. You follow your child's interests and help them dig in, without a set curriculum.
- **Charlotte Mason.** Living books instead of textbooks, narration, short lessons and plenty of nature study.
- **Unit studies.** One topic carries several subjects for a stretch of time. In Eden's Table, the topic is one plant a week.
- **Eclectic.** A mix of methods, picked for what each child needs.
- **Classical.** A rigorous approach in three stages: memorizing the basics when kids are young, reasoning in the middle grades and expressing ideas well in the upper grades.
- **Very structured programs and school at home.** At the far end are very detailed programs that lay out every week for you, some meeting weekly with other families, and school at home, built on the classroom model with textbooks, workbooks and tests.

You get to decide what works for your family and what doesn't. Sometimes it's going to be trial and error, and that's OK. Start minimal, and add as you go.
