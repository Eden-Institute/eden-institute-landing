Yes. You're qualified to homeschool your children. No state requires every homeschooling parent to be a certified teacher. I know how much weight that piece of paper seems to carry, because I had one. After twelve years in the classroom and a master's degree in education, I let my teaching certificate lapse. On purpose.

## The message I didn't get to send

At my day job, I placed a big order with an overseas manufacturer. For months, we got the runaround about a tracking number. Then one day a notification popped up: it had *just* shipped. There had never been a tracking number, because it had never gone anywhere.

I started typing, "Why did you lie to me?" My CEO stopped me. "Say, 'Why did you misinform me?'"

She didn't misinform me. She lied. And we have gotten so used to softening the truth that we'd rather use the wrong word than say the right one. That's exactly how a lot of moms have been talked out of homeschooling.

## "You're not qualified" is a lie

Here's what the Home School Legal Defense Association says: "no state requires that every homeschooling parent be a certified teacher." Some states do have basic requirements, like a high school diploma or its equivalent, so look up your state's law on [HSLDA's legal map](https://hslda.org/legal) before you start.

But a teaching certificate? Not required. HSLDA also notes that research has found little difference in achievement between homeschooled kids whose parents were certified teachers and those whose parents weren't.

I had the certificate. It was never what made me their mother.

## Why I walked away

I spent twelve years in the classroom. I was trained to teach the child who doesn't fit the system, and I watched that system tell children, over and over, that the way God made them wasn't acceptable. Too loud. Too slow. Too much. Not enough.

Keeping my certificate would have taken nothing but paperwork. But switching careers is hard, and I knew that if I left that door open, I'd run right back through it the first time things got scary. So I burned that bridge myself.

## Who's actually qualified

You are. The woman who has watched her child since the day they were born. Who knows what they're afraid of, what lights them up, and exactly what their face does when something's wrong. No college course can give you that.

What you don't know yet, you can learn right alongside them. That's not a weakness in homeschooling. That's the whole point.

## Everyday people built the wall

When Nehemiah rebuilt the walls of Jerusalem, the people who did the work weren't professional builders. Nehemiah 3 lists goldsmiths, merchants and the son of an apothecary. One man worked right alongside his girls: "Shallum the son of Hallohesh, the official of half the district of Jerusalem, made repairs, he and his daughters" (Nehemiah 3:12, NASB).

The mockers laughed at them. They built anyway, "with one hand doing the work, and the other keeping hold of a weapon" (Nehemiah 4:17, NASB). The wall was finished in fifty-two days.

They weren't qualified by the world's standards. They were faithful. That's what God used.

## Speak the truth, in love

So when someone at a playdate says, "I'd love to homeschool, but I'm not qualified," don't nod along. Scripture calls us to "speaking the truth in love" (Ephesians 4:15, NASB). Tell her the truth: she's more qualified than she thinks.

Face it. Smash it. Take it back.
