Charlotte Mason nature study means your child learns about God's creation from the real thing: a plant in their hand, a drawing in their own nature notebook, and telling back what they noticed in their own words, with as much time outdoors as you can give it. You don't need a botany degree or a shelf of field guides. You need a plant, a pencil and a little unhurried attention.

Below is what that looks like across one real week of [Eden's Table](/homeschool), the Charlotte Mason inspired curriculum I wrote, using the very first week of the year: lavender. [Week 1 is free](/freebies) if you'd like to follow along on paper.

## What did Charlotte Mason say about nature study?

She wrote about it at length in *Home Education*, first published in 1886. A few of her ideas run through everything she said about it:

- **Children learn from real things.** One of her chapters is titled "The Child Gets Knowledge by Means of His Senses." Looking, smelling and touching come before reading about it.
- **A nature notebook.** "As soon as he is able to keep it himself, a nature-diary is a source of delight to a child." She pictured it filling up from ordinary days: "three squirrels in a larch tree, a jay flying across such a field, a caterpillar climbing up a nettle."
- **Narration.** She called telling back an art every child already has: "Children Narrate by Nature." Instead of a worksheet, your child tells you what they saw.
- **The habit of attention.** She treated paying attention as a habit you grow a little every day, and she paired it with short lessons.
- **Time outside.** She passed along a simple rule of life: "Never be within doors when you can rightly be without."

And she didn't expect mothers to be experts. She wrote for the mother who "is no great botanist" and simply pointed her to a good book of wildflower pictures.

Nature study is worship, too. "Or speak to the earth, and let it teach you" (Job 12:8, NASB1995). In the Eden's Table guide, the plant always points back to the One who made it: "The same God who folded that scent into so small a flower made the very nose that breathes it in."

## How does a week of nature study run?

Every week follows the same five-day rhythm, so your children always know what comes next, and one plant carries the whole week.

**Monday: a story, Scripture and attention.** You open with Scripture and begin the memory verse. In Sprouts Week 1 it's Psalm 139:14, "I am fearfully and wonderfully made; wonderful are Your works" (NASB1995). Week 1 also opens the family read-aloud, "Meet the Family," and the guide asks you to watch how Vovó pays attention "to people, to plants, to the smallest things, the very way we will learn all year." Your child tells back who is who in the Eden family. That's narration on the very first day.

**Tuesday: Discovery, with the real plant.** This is the heart of nature study. You set a few dried lavender buds in your child's palm. They look at the purple spikes, roll a bud between two fingers, smell it, and feel the narrow gray-green leaves. The guide says to "ask your child to tell back what each sense found, in their own words; there is no wrong answer, only careful looking." Then they open the Nature Notebook, draw a lavender spike, label the flowers and the stem, and say its Latin name, *Lavandula angustifolia*.

**Wednesday: Kitchen Lab.** Wednesday is the most hands-on day of the week. In Sprouts you make one simple preparation and enjoy it the same day. Week 1 is lavender tea and a little cloth sachet: measure a teaspoon of buds, count the five minutes of steeping together, strain, cool and taste. In Seedlings the lab is a jar that has to wait. Your child writes down what she expects will happen, labels the jar with the date it opens, and finds out weeks later whether she was right.

**Thursday: History and Art.** The plant gets a place and a time. Lavender goes to ancient Rome, where its name is traditionally traced to the Latin *lavare*, "to wash." Your child finds the Mediterranean Sea and Italy on the map, adds lavender to the Creation Care Timeline, and draws three lavender stems in front of Roman arches.

**Friday: Garden and Review, outside.** Friday sends everyone out the door. Week 1 says, "Stand still in a garden for a minute and you will find it is not still at all," and asks your child to find three living things, maybe a leaf, a bug and a worm. Week 2's garden page is called "Look with new eyes": pick one plant, count its colors, and find one thing you never noticed before. A yard or a windowsill pot works just as well as a garden. Then you go over the week's review questions together and recite the memory verse.

The Seedlings year (grades 3-5) has the same five-day shape with its own 36 plants, starting with elderberry.

## What goes in a nature notebook?

Each week your child adds a page to the Nature Notebook: a drawing of the plant, a few labels and its name. For a K-2 child it's mostly drawing and telling back. By the end of the year it's a full year of your child's work, dated and in order, which takes care of a lot of your record keeping too. In Seedlings the pages grow into measurements, ratios and a hypothesis your child writes and then tests.

If you'd like more of Charlotte Mason's everyday diary in your week, bring the Notebook outside on Friday and let your child draw whatever they find out there.

## How long should nature study take?

Not long. Charlotte Mason paired attention with short lessons, and the Eden's Table guide says it the same way: "Plan a short, unhurried session each day. If a week needs two, take two. This is a feast, not a race."

Every week also has a short chant and a memory verse your children say together, so the plant's name and the Scripture stay with them long after the week is over.

## Can I do nature study with more than one child?

Yes, and this is where nature study shines. Every lesson has a line for younger children and a line for older children. On the Week 1 garden day, Little Sprouts find one living thing and name it, and Older Sprouts draw all three and tell what each one was doing. A five-year-old and an eight-year-old do the same lesson at their own level, at the same table.

## How is Eden's Table different from classic Charlotte Mason nature study?

Classic Charlotte Mason nature study follows the seasons and whatever your child finds outside that day. Eden's Table is planned for you: one plant a week for 36 weeks, in six units, with a kitchen lab every Wednesday.

Each week uses a small amount of a common dried herb from your pantry, your garden or the [herb list](/homeschool/herbs). Anything you prepare comes from herbs bought from a reputable source or grown in your own garden, never wild plants. So the close study of the week's plant happens at the table, and the outdoor part happens on Friday and on your own walks.

You can use it as your science and nature study, or add it alongside what's already working, like an [AmblesideOnline](https://www.amblesideonline.org/) year. You can see every plant for both years on the [scope and sequence](/homeschool/scope-and-sequence) page.
