## 3. Family Map: your teaching wiring

From your own questions (approved this morning). Shows the blocks for YOUR top 3 wirings, then one line for each of the 4 sides of your teaching style, picked by your result (leans one way, a balance, or leans the other way).

### Your teaching wiring: the opening

You're wired too. Here's what you reach for, and how you like to teach. None of it is right or wrong: it's how God made you to lead your home school. Your top 3 wirings are below, then the four sides of your teaching style.

### A Storyteller mom

You probably love read-alouds, narration and talking things through. Your kids hear a lot of good words, and that's a gift.
Keep in mind: a child who needs to move or work quietly may tire of long talks, so give them a way in too.

### A Organizer mom

You bring order: a plan, a schedule, clear steps. Your kids know what to expect, and that helps them settle.
Keep in mind: some days won't follow the plan. A child who wanders off on a rabbit trail may be learning plenty.

### A Designer mom

You notice how things look and fit together: a lovely space, a good diagram, a well-made map. You help your kids see.
Keep in mind: a child who doesn't think in pictures may need words or hands to get there instead.

### A Maker mom

You'd rather do than talk about doing: projects, kitchen science, real work. Your kids learn that school can happen anywhere.
Keep in mind: a quieter child may need some still time and some reading before they jump in.

### A Psalmist mom

Music runs through your day: hymns, songs, rhythm. Your home sounds like worship, and your kids remember what they sing.
Keep in mind: a child who needs quiet to focus may do better with music saved for its own time.

### A Connector mom

You teach through relationship: talking, co-op, doing it together. Your kids feel known.
Keep in mind: a child who recharges alone may need time on their own before and after group days.

### A Ponderer mom

You think things through and give your kids room to think too. You're often the calm in the room.
Keep in mind: a child who processes out loud may need you to talk it through with them, even when you'd rather mull it over.

### A Cultivator mom

You'd teach outside every day if you could: nature walks, gardens, animals. Your kids learn to notice God's creation.
Keep in mind: a child who loves indoor work may need you to bring some of it inside, too.

### A Seeker mom

You keep asking why, and you lead your kids to big questions and to Scripture. They learn that faith and learning go together.
Keep in mind: a younger or more practical child may need the answer first and the big questions later.

### Structure: what each result says

- leans toward a steady routine: You love a routine, and it gives your kids a sense of security. Build in a little wiggle room for the days that go sideways.
- a balance of both: You mix routine and flexibility, adjusting as the day needs.
- leans toward going with the flow: You go with the flow, and your kids get room to follow a spark. A few anchors, like the same start and the same end to the day, keep things from drifting.

### Planning: what each result says

- leans toward planning ahead: You plan ahead, so open-and-go curriculum and a weekly planning hour will feel natural.
- a balance of both: You plan the big pieces and leave the details for the day.
- leans toward deciding in the moment: You decide in the moment. Choose materials that need little prep, and keep a short list of go-to activities for days with no plan.

### Capacity: what each result says

- has room to spare right now: You have room to spare right now, so this may be a good season to add a family project or a new subject.
- a balance of both: Your energy is steady but not endless, so protect it: keep family subjects together and let older kids work on their own.
- is running close to empty right now: Your tank is running low right now. That's a season, not a verdict. Lean on open-and-go materials, family subjects and a shorter school day for a while, and ask for help. "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28).

### Resistance: what each result says

- leans toward finding another way in: When a child pushes back, you look for another way in. Keep a few clear expectations too, so they know what stays the same.
- a balance of both: You flex when it helps and hold steady when it matters.
- leans toward steady follow-through: You help your kids finish what they start, and that builds perseverance. When frustration is high, naming the feeling first can help them get back to work.

## 4. Family Map: together, weekly rhythm, siblings

The three family pages. Bracketed words fill in from your family's answers.

### Teaching your kids together

Teaching several kids doesn't mean several separate schools. "Now there are diversities of gifts, but the same Spirit" (1 Corinthians 12:4). Teach the family subjects together and give each child their own way in.
- Family subjects you can teach all together: Bible, read-alouds, history, science, nature study, art, music and poetry. Each child works at their own level afterward.
- Wirings your kids share: [Wiring] shows up in [Names]. Teach [a subject] together through it (for example, a shared nature walk for Cultivators).
- Where they differ: in a family lesson, give each child their own job. The Storyteller narrates, the Designer draws, the Maker builds the model.
- You and each child: you're a [Your top wiring]; [Nickname] is a [Their top wiring]. When the two differ, offer [Nickname] a way in that isn't your own favorite.
- Skills subjects (reading, writing, math) stay separate by level. Stagger them so you sit with one child while another works on their own.

### Your family's week

Here's a family week to start with, built from your answers. "Let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not" (Galatians 6:9).
- Every morning: family time together (Bible, a read-aloud, a hymn or memory work).
- Then: skills time, staggered. Sit with your youngest first while older kids start on their own checklist.
- Two or three afternoons: a family subject (history, science or nature study) everyone does together.
- One day a week: outside or out of the house (nature walk, field trip, co-op, park day).
- End of the week: a family project or show-and-tell, where each child shares something in their own way.
- If your energy is low right now, try a 4-day week and keep only the family morning and skills time on the lightest days.
- If you lean toward planning ahead, plan this week on the weekend. If you lean toward the moment, plan only Monday morning and let the rest follow. (Camila 2026-11-21 4:29 PM CT, tap "Whole line, every mom": both sentences show to every mom, whatever her planning result.)

### How your kids' wirings work together

Brothers and sisters are wired differently on purpose. "Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity!" (Psalm 133:1).
- When siblings share a top wiring: they'll love doing it together, and they may also compete. Give each one their own corner of it.
- When one is busy and one is quiet (a Maker or Connector next to a Ponderer): the busy one needs room to move and talk, the quiet one needs a place to retreat. Neither one is the problem.
- When one loves order and one loves to explore (an Organizer next to a Maker or Cultivator): let the Organizer plan the project and the explorer test it.
- When one talks it out and one thinks it through (a Storyteller or Connector next to a Ponderer): let the talker go first at dinner, and give the thinker time to answer later.
- Name each child's gifts out loud, in front of the others. Children who feel seen compete less for your attention.
