## 2. Subject-by-subject plan

Five subjects: math, language arts, science, history and geography, Bible. Each subject page shows the ways in for the child's top 3 wirings, under the mom's own goal for the year. Built on backwards design (Wiggins and McTighe, 1998): goal first, then how you'll know, then the lessons.

### Subject-by-subject plan: the opening and the fill-in lines

For each subject, start at the end. Write one or two goals for the year at the top of the page, then how you'll know [Nickname] got there. The ideas underneath are ways in for [Nickname]'s top wirings. Use the ones that fit and skip the rest. A Storyteller still needs math facts; this just gives you doors [Nickname] already likes to walk through.
Each subject page starts with these fill-in lines:
- By next summer, I want [Nickname] to... (your goal)
- I'll know they got there when they can... (explain it, show it, or use it)
- Ways in for [Nickname]:

### Storyteller: ways in, subject by subject

- Math: Turn problems into little stories, ask [Nickname] to explain their thinking out loud, and add math picture books or living math books.
- Language arts: Narrate after every reading, copy favorite sentences from good books, and let them write and 'publish' their own stories.
- Science: Read living science books and narrate them, keep a science journal in words, and tell back how an experiment went, like a story.
- History and geography: Lean on living books and biographies, narrate history as a story, and write a diary entry as someone from the past.
- Bible: Read Scripture aloud together and narrate it, memorize whole passages, and retell Bible stories to a younger sibling.

### Organizer: ways in, subject by subject

- Math: Show the why behind each rule, add logic puzzles, and keep a notebook of patterns they find.
- Language arts: Treat grammar as a system (sorting words, diagramming sentences), learn spelling patterns, and outline before writing.
- Science: Run experiments with a question, a prediction and a result, make charts and graphs, and classify rocks, leaves or animals.
- History and geography: Build timelines, chart causes and effects, and compare maps from different times.
- Bible: Make a Bible timeline, learn the books in order, and study how a Psalm or a Proverb is put together.

### Designer: ways in, subject by subject

- Math: Draw the problem, use graph paper, tangrams and geometry, and show fractions and numbers with pictures and number lines.
- Language arts: Picture the scene before writing, use story maps, and illustrate their own narrations.
- Science: Keep a nature journal with drawings, label diagrams, and build models.
- History and geography: Draw maps from memory, build a model of a castle or a city, and make an illustrated timeline.
- Bible: Draw scenes from the reading, map Paul's journeys, and sketch the tabernacle from the measurements in Exodus.

### Maker: ways in, subject by subject

- Math: Use hands-on counters and blocks, measure, weigh and cook, and play math games that move (hopscotch facts).
- Language arts: Act out stories, use letter tiles for little ones, and break writing into short bursts with a chance to move in between.
- Science: Let them do the experiments with their own hands: building, gardening, taking things apart, field trips.
- History and geography: Build models, reenact events, and make something from the time period (bread, candles, a simple tool).
- Bible: Act out Bible stories, build the ark or the temple with blocks, and serve someone with their hands.

### Psalmist: ways in, subject by subject

- Math: Sing skip-counting songs, clap or chant math facts, and connect fractions to beats and notes.
- Language arts: Read and recite poetry, read aloud with expression, and use songs for spelling or grammar rules.
- Science: Learn facts through songs, listen for bird songs and name them, and try sound and vibration experiments.
- History and geography: Listen to music from the time period, learn the stories behind hymns, and sing folk songs from other countries.
- Bible: Sing the Psalms, study one hymn a month, and memorize verses set to music.

### Connector: ways in, subject by subject

- Math: Play math games with siblings or friends, let them teach a younger sibling, and plan something real with people in mind (a party budget).
- Language arts: Try a book club or reading buddy, write letters to real people, and talk about books together.
- Science: Do group experiments at co-op, interview someone who works in science, and let them teach the family what they learned.
- History and geography: Read biographies of real people, interview grandparents about the past, and find a pen pal in another country.
- Bible: Hold family devotions with real discussion, study the Bible with a friend, and serve together.

### Ponderer: ways in, subject by subject

- Math: Give them time to work problems alone, ask them to explain the method they chose, and keep a math journal.
- Language arts: Journal, protect quiet reading time, and let them narrate in writing when they'd rather not speak.
- Science: Try long, quiet observation (one tree through the seasons), add reflection questions, and offer independent projects on a topic they choose.
- History and geography: Think through the choices people made, read independently, and keep a book of favorite quotes.
- Bible: Keep a quiet time with a journal, meditate on one verse a week, and write out prayers.

### Cultivator: ways in, subject by subject

- Math: Count, measure and graph things in nature, do garden math (rows, area, growth charts), and sort collections.
- Language arts: Write about nature, read field guides and nature poetry, and narrate a nature walk.
- Science: Make nature study the center: nature journals, gardening, animal care, collections of rocks, shells or leaves.
- History and geography: Learn how people farmed and lived on the land, study places through their animals and climates, and explore local history outdoors.
- Bible: Study the plants and animals of the Bible, read the creation account, and talk through Jesus' parables about seeds and soil.

### Seeker: ways in, subject by subject

- Math: Talk about the why behind math and the order God built into numbers and patterns (like the spirals in a pinecone).
- Language arts: Read books that raise big questions, write persuasive pieces, and keep a wondering list to write about.
- Science: Explore big questions about creation with Scripture alongside, and talk through right and wrong in how science is used.
- History and geography: Ask why things happened and what people believed, read church history, and read missionary biographies.
- Bible: Wrestle with hard questions together, work through a catechism or Q&A, and let them lead a family devotion.

## 3. Daily rhythm and setup

One rhythm per grade band, then a setup tip for the child's top wiring. No clock times or hour counts: each family fits it to its own day.

### Daily rhythm and setup: the opening

There's no one right schedule. Here's a simple rhythm to start with, shaped by [Nickname]'s grade and top wiring. Change it as you go.

### Grades K-2

Start with Bible and a read-aloud together. Keep skills lessons (reading, writing, math) short and stop while [Nickname] is still fresh, with breaks in between. Save the afternoon for play, outdoor time and one hands-on project.

### Grades 3-5

Start with Bible and a family read-aloud. Do skills lessons (math, writing, spelling) while minds are fresh. Do science and history together as a family when you can. Leave afternoons for interests, chores and time outside.

### Grades 6-8

Give [Nickname] a checklist they manage, with more work on their own. Math, writing and reading every day; science and history on a rotation; one project or field trip a week. Check in at the start and the end of each day.

### Grades 9-12

Build a weekly plan together with high school credits in mind. Independent work with a daily check-in, room for a job, volunteering or an apprenticeship, and one family read-aloud or discussion each week.

### Setting up your space, by top wiring

- Storyteller: A reading nook, a basket of good books within reach, and a notebook that's only theirs.
- Organizer: A clear checklist or planner, labeled bins, and a whiteboard with the week on it.
- Designer: Art supplies and graph paper within reach, wall space for maps and drawings, and good light.
- Maker: Room to move, a table you don't mind getting messy, a project bin, and time outside.
- Psalmist: A spot for an instrument, a hymn playlist, and a time each day to sing.
- Connector: School at the kitchen table where people are, and one co-op or friend day each week.
- Ponderer: A quiet corner of their own, a journal, and time alone built into the day.
- Cultivator: A nature shelf for their finds, a window bird feeder, plants they care for, and time outside every day.
- Seeker: A wondering list on the fridge, a Bible and a few big-question books within reach, and time to talk at dinner.

### Where the ideas come from

- Backwards design: Wiggins and McTighe, Understanding by Design (ASCD, 1998); three stages: identify desired results, determine acceptable evidence, plan learning experiences (University of Toronto summary, read 2026-11-19).
- Interest change: Pässler and Hell (2020), already in the plan's research basis: ages 11 to 14 show the most change in interests.
- Ways in are about giving interests room (Harackiewicz and others, 2016, approved with the results page), never about how a child learns (Pashler and others, 2008).
