## 2. Best-fit approach

Each approach gets matched to the wirings it gives the most room to, based on what that approach puts first. The page shows the approach with the most room for the child's strongest wirings (an average, so an approach with a long list can't win just by size), plus a second one if it's close. Charlotte Mason has the longest list here, and Eden's Table is Charlotte Mason, so averaging keeps this fair.

### Which approach gives room to which wirings

- Charlotte Mason: Living books, narration, nature study, art and music, in short lessons. Room for: Storyteller, Cultivator, Psalmist, Ponderer, Designer.
- Classical: Learning in stages, with memory work, great books and lots of good questions. Room for: Organizer, Storyteller, Seeker.
- Traditional / textbook: Clear lessons in order, with a textbook and workbook for each subject that a child can work through on their own. Room for: Organizer, Ponderer.
- Unit study: One theme ties many subjects together, often with projects the whole family does at once. Room for: Connector, Maker, Designer.
- Literature-based: Great books carry history, reading and more. Room for: Storyteller, Seeker, Ponderer.
- Montessori: Hands-on materials and child-led work in a calm, ordered space. Room for: Maker, Designer, Organizer, Ponderer.
- Waldorf: Art, music, handwork and the rhythm of the seasons. Room for: Psalmist, Designer, Maker, Cultivator.
- Eclectic / mixed: Mix and match from several approaches. Often the best fit for a Renaissance profile, where many wirings are strong at once.
- Interest-led (unschooling) and Online / video are not matched to wirings: interest-led follows any child's passions, and online is a format, not an approach.

## 3. Three things to try this week

Heading on the page: 3 things to try this week. The tips come from the child's top wiring. Your answer to 'biggest frustration' picks which tip shows first (you approved that this morning): keeping them interested or 'are we doing enough' leads with Try today; time or budget leads with Low prep; teaching several ages or picking curriculum leads with Together.

### Storyteller: the 3 tips

- Try today: Read aloud together tonight, then ask [Nickname] to tell the story back in their own words.
- Low prep or free: Keep a library basket by the couch and a notebook that's just theirs. Words cost almost nothing and need almost no prep.
- Together, or choosing curriculum: Look for curriculum built on real books and narration. Siblings can share the read-aloud, and [Nickname] can be the one who tells it back.

### Organizer: the 3 tips

- Try today: Give [Nickname] a real job that needs order: the pantry shelf, the chore chart, or this week's school checklist.
- Low prep or free: Card games, dominoes and puzzles count as math practice, and most homes already have them.
- Together, or choosing curriculum: Look for curriculum that shows the why behind each step. With siblings, let [Nickname] keep the schedule or the score.

### Designer: the 3 tips

- Try today: Hand [Nickname] paper and colored pencils during read-aloud, and let them sketch what they hear.
- Low prep or free: Blocks, Legos, a sketchbook and the backyard make a free design studio. Ask for a map of your street or a plan for their room.
- Together, or choosing curriculum: Look for curriculum with maps, diagrams, timelines and drawing built in. Siblings can build a project while [Nickname] draws the plan.

### Maker: the 3 tips

- Try today: Before seatwork, let [Nickname] move: a lap around the yard, kneading dough, or building the lesson with their hands.
- Low prep or free: Cooking, fixing, gardening and chores are hands-on lessons that cost nothing extra. Measure the recipe, count the screws.
- Together, or choosing curriculum: Look for curriculum with experiments, projects and things to build, not only worksheets. Makers often lead the hands-on part for younger siblings.

### Psalmist: the 3 tips

- Try today: Put a Bible verse or a list you're learning to a simple tune, and let [Nickname] sing it back to you.
- Low prep or free: Play hymns or classical music while you work. A free playlist and an old hymnal are all you need.
- Together, or choosing curriculum: Look for curriculum with songs, poems and rhythm in it. The whole family can sing the same verse together.

### Connector: the 3 tips

- Try today: Let [Nickname] teach you or a sibling what they just learned. Connectors light up when there's someone to share it with.
- Low prep or free: A weekly park day, co-op or library group gives a Connector time with people without adding prep for you.
- Together, or choosing curriculum: Look for curriculum you can do together at the table, with discussion and group projects. Connectors are often the glue between siblings.

### Ponderer: the 3 tips

- Try today: After a lesson, give [Nickname] a few quiet minutes to think or journal before you ask questions.
- Low prep or free: A notebook and a cozy corner cost almost nothing. Ponderers often do their deepest thinking alone.
- Together, or choosing curriculum: Look for curriculum with short lessons and room for independent work. With siblings, give [Nickname] a quiet spot for part of the day.

### Cultivator: the 3 tips

- Try today: Take today's lesson outside: a nature walk, a leaf to identify, a bird to look up together.
- Low prep or free: Your backyard, a window bird feeder and a library field guide make a free science lab.
- Together, or choosing curriculum: Look for curriculum with nature study, gardening or animals built in. Siblings of every age can share one nature walk and journal at their own level.

### Seeker: the 3 tips

- Try today: Start a wondering list for [Nickname]'s big questions, and look for answers in Scripture together.
- Low prep or free: Your Bible, a few great books and good talks at the table cost nothing and feed a Seeker well.
- Together, or choosing curriculum: Look for curriculum that talks about the why: worldview, character and the Bible's big story. Family devotions can be where [Nickname] asks the questions.

## 6. Not a diagnostic tool, evaluations, and the fine print

The honest-limits section every results page carries, plus the affiliate and children's privacy wording. Every fact here comes from the spec you approved or from the sources listed at the end.

### Not a diagnostic tool

Heading: What this quiz is, and isn't
This quiz shows the strengths and interests you notice in [Nickname] right now. It is not a diagnostic tool. It doesn't measure intelligence, it isn't a test score, and it can't find or rule out a learning difference, an attention issue or anything medical.
It also doesn't sort [Nickname] into a type or tell you how [Nickname] takes in information. Research hasn't found that matching teaching to a child's type helps children learn more.
Kids grow and change, especially in the teen years, so treat this as a snapshot and take it again each July.

### When to look into an evaluation

Heading: When to look into an evaluation
Every child struggles sometimes. Talk with [Nickname]'s doctor if [Nickname] keeps having a hard time for months, even with steady teaching, with things like reading, spelling or sounding out words, math, following directions, staying focused, or getting organized. Do the same if you notice speech, hearing or vision concerns, or your mom gut says something is off. Experts say getting help early is best.
At well visits, ask whether [Nickname] is due for the routine vision and hearing checks.
Under federal law, public school districts must find and evaluate children who may have a disability, at no cost to parents. Whether that includes homeschooled children depends on your state, so call your local school district and ask. Nobody can make you agree to an evaluation.

### Where these facts come from

- Type-matching: Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008), Psychological Science in the Public Interest 9(3): 'no adequate evidence base' for matching instruction to styles; Aslaksen & Loras (2018), Frontiers in Psychology, agrees. A 2024 meta-analysis found only a small, inconsistent effect, so the page says 'hasn't found', not 'proven false'.
- Interest: Harackiewicz, Smith & Priniski (2016), Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences: interest is marked by 'increased attention, effort, and affect' and relates to persistence.
- Evaluations: 34 CFR 300.111 and 300.131 (districts must locate, identify and evaluate; cost not charged to parents); US Dept. of Education, 71 Fed. Reg. 46594 (2006): homeschool coverage 'is a matter left to State law'; 34 CFR 300.300(d)(4): for homeschooled children the district may not override a parent's no; Center for Parent Information and Resources: evaluation 'at no cost to parents'.
- Signs to watch: American Academy of Pediatrics, healthychildren.org (2024, learning disabilities); CDC (ADHD signs: when symptoms continue and cause difficulty at school, home or with friends); ASHA ('get help with LD as early as possible'); AAP Bright Futures schedule (2025) for routine vision and hearing screening ages.
- Disclosures: FTC Endorsement Guides FAQ ('The closer the disclosure is to your recommendation, the better'); FTC COPPA FAQ (covers information collected online from children under 13, not information from adults; neutral age screens).
